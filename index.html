From Storage to Logistics Intelligence

Hydrogen storage is usually treated as a tank problem. This project treats it as a coordination problem across a local ecosystem, and uses a digital twin of production, trailers and users to show how much storage coordination can replace.

Research questionHow can digital twins help local hydrogen systems produce, move and use hydrogen with minimum storage and maximum business value?

1 · The problem

Why "where do we store it?" is the wrong first question

Hydrogen is hard to keep. It is the lightest gas, so even compressed to 500 bar a kilogram needs roughly thirty litres of pressure vessel, and liquefaction costs a large share of the energy it carries (IEA, 2019). Local hydrogen systems in the Nordics therefore tend to answer every uncertainty with another tank. The difficulty is that most of that uncertainty is not physical. It sits between organisations.

Physical level

Low volumetric density, high pressure, safety distances and permits make every kilogram of stationary storage expensive to build and slow to approve (Papadias & Ahluwalia, 2021). Cold climates add their own problems, from fuel-cell cold starts to efficiency losses (Luo & Jiao, 2018).

Ecosystem level

Producers, hauliers and users each size buffers for their own risk. Nobody sees the whole chain: wind and price forecasts sit with the producer, demand plans with the bus operator or the port, truck positions with the haulier. Each tank protects one actor against the others' uncertainty.

Strategic level

Early markets have a chicken-and-egg problem: users wait for secure supply, suppliers wait for demand. Storage becomes the price of that mistrust. Supply chain research calls for planning across production, storage, distribution and use as one system (Sgarbossa et al., 2023).

In logistics, a store is what you build when supply and demand cannot be synchronised. The question is not only how to build cheaper tanks, but how to need fewer of them.

What the literature already shows

  • Storage and delivery can dominate the cost of supplied hydrogen, and the cheapest delivery mode depends on distance and volume (Yang & Ogden, 2007; Reuss et al., 2017).
  • Trucks used as mobile storage give production extra flexibility in time and space and make electrolysis more competitive (He et al., 2021).
  • Inventory routing can absorb intermittent green hydrogen supply through scheduling rather than capacity (Hastürk et al., 2024; Coelho et al., 2014).
  • There is a real trade-off: small on-site systems are more efficient, large chains gain from storing cheap power (Stöckl et al., 2021).

What is still missing

  • Digital twins in hydrogen are mostly built for single assets: an electrolyser, a fuel cell, a station. Twins of the flow between sites are rare.
  • Storage avoidance is seldom a stated design objective, and the trade-off between forecast quality and storage capacity is rarely priced.
  • Nordic winter conditions, and the managerial side of sharing data between actors, are thinly studied.

These gaps come from an ongoing systematic literature review by the author on digital twins as logistics intelligence in the hydrogen value chain.

Nordic context. Finland's national hydrogen roadmap (Laurikko et al., 2020) and the planned Nordic Hydrogen Route around the Bay of Bothnia (Gasgrid Finland, n.d.) point to large-scale hydrogen. Many early users, however, will be local: bus depots, truck stations on the TEN-T corridors, where the EU requires refuelling stations every 200 km by 2030 (Regulation (EU) 2023/1804), industrial sites and ports. These local systems are where storage decisions are made first.

4 · The digital twin

What the twin sees, decides and sends back

The twin is not a 3D model of a tank. It is a model of the whole flow of hydrogen, kept in step with live data and used to make decisions every hour. Click a layer.

Four ways to run the same hardware

The simulation compares four operating strategies. The last three use exactly the same small tanks and the same seven trailers; they differ only in how much they know and how far they look ahead. This follows the model, shadow and twin distinction of Kritzinger et al. (2018).

Storage-firstBuffer everythingThree days of tanks at every user, two tonnes at each electrolyser. Produce when power is cheaper than last week's average. Reorder when a tank is half empty.
Digital modelFixed timetableSmall tanks. Steady production and a weekly delivery timetable planned from average demand. A site that runs dry phones in.
Digital shadowMonitoring onlySmall tanks. Live tank levels trigger deliveries at a reorder point, but nobody looks ahead at weather, prices or events.
Digital twinPlan, act, re-planSmall tanks. A rolling 48-hour plan from forecasts: deliveries timed to when each site will need them, production scheduled backwards from those deliveries into the cheapest hours, a reserve kept on wheels.

How to read the result

Service level is the share of hydrogen demand supplied on time. "Hours without fuel" counts hours in which any user could not be served. Hydrogen held is the average amount in tanks and trailers. Cost per kg covers electricity, compression, trucking, the annualised cost of stationary tanks and the change in stock. Margin adds sales and subtracts penalties for unmet demand. Electrolysers and trailers are the same in every strategy, so their capital cost is left out of the comparison.

8 · Business value

Where the value comes from, and who captures it

Less idle capital

Tanks that are no longer needed are capital, land and permits that are not spent. In the default month the storage-first design ties up more than ten times the stationary capacity of the twin.

Cheaper electricity

A plan that knows when deliveries are due can put production into cheap, windy hours without building a tank to hold the result. The reserve lives in trailers that have to exist anyway.

Reliability as a service

Users pay for a promise of supply. The twin keeps that promise through cold snaps, calm spells, storms and trips, which is what makes long-term contracts, and therefore investment, possible.

Performance measures for a storage-minimised system

If storage is a logistics outcome, it needs logistics measures. These are the indicators the twin reports every hour, and the ones a performance management system for such an ecosystem would track.

  • Storage intensity: kg of stationary storage per kg of daily demand.
  • Hydrogen days of cover: hydrogen held divided by forecast demand.
  • Service level and hours without fuel, per user.
  • Delivered cost per kg, split into power, compression, trucking and storage.
  • Share of production in cheap hours and the average power price paid.
  • Trailer utilisation and kilometres per kg delivered.
9 · Method and assumptions

How the model works, and what it does not claim

Model

Hourly discrete-time simulation over 720 hours. Synthetic weather (wind capacity factor and temperature as mean-reverting processes), spot prices driven by wind, time of day and cold, and six stress events. Two electrolysers fill tube trailers; seven trailers and three tractors run drop-and-swap trips; four users draw hydrogen from the docked trailer and a small on-site tank. The twin sees forecasts whose error grows with lead time (the forecast error slider scales it), sees the road storm 12 hours ahead, and does not foresee the electrolyser trip. The code is in sim.js and runs identically in the browser and in Node.

Limitations

  • Illustrative data, not a real site. Prices, costs and demand are plausible, not measured.
  • The twin uses transparent rules (backward scheduling, greedy cheapest-hour allocation), not a full mathematical optimisation, so its results are a lower bound on what a twin can do.
  • One region, one month. Seasonal storage, pipelines and liquid carriers are outside the scope.
  • Trailer capacity is itself storage. The claim is not that storage disappears, but that shared, mobile capacity replaces dedicated tanks.
